          How this check works

          Exposure means possible effects on tasks. Holding means reasons a person may still be needed. Neither is a measure of job security.

          The assessment gives priority to actual job cuts, then unfilled vacancies. With four or more unknown answers it recommends gathering information. Otherwise it considers digital tasks alongside adoption and specific reasons for human involvement: personal required sign-off, physical work, or substantial judgment combined with final review or personal accountability. These are editorial rules, not validated predictions. The detail remains available under the result.

          The first six task choices involve digital output that generative AI may assist with. The next three involve working with people or making decisions; the final three involve physical work or care. The result describes the groups you select and keeps their differences visible. It does not estimate how much of your week could be automated.

          The other questions add context: review, error costs, required sign-off, physical work, accountability, judgment, AI use, and staffing changes. They do not add or subtract points. A license does not guarantee a job, AI use does not prove job losses, and staff reductions do not establish their cause. “Not sure” stays unknown.

          This is Jim’s practical checklist, not a validated prediction model. The ILO–NASK study, May 2025, provides background on task exposure and the distinction between job transformation and replacement. Its occupational scores are not used here, and it does not validate this checklist’s task groups.
